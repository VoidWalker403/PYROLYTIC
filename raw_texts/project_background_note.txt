=== PYROLYSIS FUNDAMENTALS ===

Pyrolysis is the thermal decomposition of a material in the absence of oxygen. For waste
plastic, heating breaks long polymer chains into smaller molecules, producing three main
product streams: oil (condensable liquid hydrocarbons), gas (non-condensable hydrocarbons like
methane, ethylene, propylene), and char (solid carbon residue and inorganic residue).

Temperature effect: at lower temperatures (300-450C), primary cracking dominates, producing
mostly larger liquid-range molecules (higher oil yield). At higher temperatures (550C+),
secondary cracking becomes significant — larger oil molecules further break down into smaller
gas molecules, so oil yield typically peaks at a moderate temperature and declines above it,
while gas yield keeps rising with temperature.

Heating rate effect: slow heating rates give molecules more time to undergo controlled primary
cracking reactions, often favoring liquid oil yield. Fast heating rates can favor different
reaction pathways and gas formation, though the exact effect is reactor- and plastic-specific
(this project's own dataset shows this varies significantly by feedstock).

Residence time: longer residence time at pyrolysis temperature allows more secondary cracking
of vapors before they exit the reactor and condense, generally shifting yield from oil toward
gas.

=== REACTOR TYPES ===

Batch reactor: feedstock is loaded once, heated through a full temperature program, then
unloaded. Simple, common in lab-scale studies, but not continuous (not directly suited to
industrial throughput without modification).

Semi-batch reactor: similar to batch, but may have continuous removal of gas/vapor products
while solid feedstock remains batch-loaded.

Fixed-bed reactor: feedstock sits in a stationary bed while heat and/or gas flows through it.
Common for both lab-scale and some industrial designs.

Fluidized-bed reactor: feedstock particles are suspended and mixed by an upward gas flow,
giving very good heat transfer and temperature uniformity. Preferred for larger-scale/
industrial continuous operation due to consistent product quality, though it has higher
capital cost and complexity than simpler batch designs.

Continuous/pilot-scale reactors: designed for ongoing feedstock input and product output
(rather than one batch at a time), necessary for realistic industrial throughput (e.g. the
5 kg/h and 5 kg feedstock pilot-scale sources in this project's dataset).

=== CATALYSTS IN PLASTIC PYROLYSIS ===

Catalysts are used to lower the required reaction temperature, improve selectivity toward
desired products (e.g. more oil, less gas), or upgrade oil quality (e.g. more valuable
aromatic compounds).

Zeolites (e.g. ZSM-5, HMOR, HUSY): microporous aluminosilicate catalysts with strong acid
sites; widely studied for plastic pyrolysis because they can crack larger hydrocarbons
selectively and produce more valuable, narrower product ranges. Different zeolite types and
pore structures give different product selectivity.

Natural minerals (e.g. kaolin, natural silica/sand): lower-cost alternatives to synthetic
zeolites, generally milder catalytic effect, but attractive for economic reasons at scale.

Catalyst-to-plastic ratio: the mass ratio of catalyst to feedstock is a key operating variable
— too little catalyst gives minimal effect, too much increases cost without proportional
yield benefit; most studies test a range (e.g. 1:8 to 1:1 in this project's dataset) to find
an economic optimum.

=== TECHNO-ECONOMIC TERMS (relevant to this project's economics module) ===

CAPEX (Capital Expenditure): the upfront cost to build a plant — equipment, construction,
installation. Usually reported per unit of processing capacity (e.g. $/tonne-year).

OPEX (Operating Expenditure): the ongoing annual cost to run the plant — labor, maintenance,
utilities, insurance. Usually reported per tonne processed or per year.

NPV (Net Present Value): the sum of all future cash flows from a project, discounted back to
today's value, minus the initial investment. A positive NPV means the project is expected to
be profitable in present-value terms; the higher the NPV, the more value the project creates.

IRR (Internal Rate of Return): the discount rate at which a project's NPV equals zero — in
plain terms, the effective annual percentage return the investment generates. Compared against
a company's required rate of return (hurdle rate) to judge if a project is worth pursuing.

Payback period: the time required for cumulative cash flow to recover the initial investment.
Shorter payback generally means lower risk, though it doesn't account for profitability after
that point the way NPV does.

Sensitivity analysis: testing how much a project's financial outcome (e.g. NPV) changes when
key assumptions (e.g. product price, feedstock cost) are varied — used to identify which
assumptions the investment decision is most vulnerable to.

=== DATA SCIENCE / MODELING TERMS (relevant to this project's methodology) ===

R² (R-squared): a statistic between 0 and 1 (can be negative for a poorly-fit model) showing
how much of the variation in the target variable a model explains. Higher is generally better,
but a high R² on training data alone can be misleading (overfitting) — this project uses
cross-validation specifically to guard against that.

Cross-validation: a technique where a dataset is split into multiple parts, and a model is
trained on some parts and tested on the held-out part, repeated across different splits. This
gives a more honest estimate of how a model will perform on new, unseen data than testing on
the same data it was trained on.

Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV): an extreme form of cross-validation where each single
data point is held out as the test set once, and the model is trained on all remaining points.
Used in this project for very small datasets (e.g. the 16-row PP subset) where standard
k-fold cross-validation would leave too few points per fold.

Confounding variable: a variable that correlates with both the predictor and the outcome in a
way that creates a misleading appearance of a relationship. This project specifically
identified and diagnosed a confound where a model appeared to predict yield well, but was
actually partly learning "which research paper the data came from" rather than genuine
chemistry — documented in detail in this project's model card.

Box-Behnken Design / Response Surface Methodology (RSM): a structured statistical experimental
design that tests combinations of factors (e.g. temperature, time, catalyst ratio) at three
levels each, without needing to test every possible combination, in order to efficiently map
out how those factors affect an outcome (like oil yield) including curved (non-linear)
relationships. Several of the primary data sources in this project's dataset used this design.
